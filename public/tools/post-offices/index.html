Coverage. The panel records an office only in the years it appears in the statement, so the growth from 629 offices in 1896 to 1,487 in 1910 is the spread of free city delivery. A measure can still be blank in a year an office is present: 1910 carries receipts for only about a quarter of offices, and horses and cars were recorded through 1902. Offices present but missing the chosen measure are drawn as hollow grey rings.

Boundaries. State outlines are the modern Census 20m generalized file, an approximation for this period — Oklahoma was admitted in 1907, Arizona and New Mexico in 1912. Hawaii and Puerto Rico are drawn as insets; they hold four offices (Honolulu, San Juan, Ponce and Mayagüez).

Post offices and the railroad network

Railroads. Suggested citation for the network data: Jeremy Atack, “Historical Geographic Information Systems (GIS) database of U.S. Railroads for 1830–1910” (May 2016; revised October 2023). The bracketed selection in Atack's suggested citation is filled from the criterion used here: every segment whose InOpBy field is 1910 or earlier — 69,476 segments and 195,509 route-miles out of the 76,849 in Atack's full Railroads shapefile.

The layer is cumulative, and it is not annual. Each year shows the track in operation that year plus every earlier year's, so the network only ever grows. But InOpBy is cross-sectional: Atack digitised a set of map years, so within this window the network really moves only twice — 1898 adds 11,325 route-miles and 1903 adds 14,937. The remaining years add a handful of segments each, and 1899, 1901 and 1902 add none. The caption above always names the vintage actually drawn, which is why it can lag the year on the slider.

Reading the map. Track already in operation when the panel opens in 1896 is drawn in grey; track added between 1897 and 1910 is drawn in orange. Post offices sit on top, sized and coloured by the same measures as the Explorer tab, and the two tabs share one year and one selection. Choosing a state zooms both the offices and the network; track continuing across the state line is drawn as it runs.

Twenty largest counties

What this tab is for. The other two tabs are about what the Post Office Department did. This one asks whether it mattered. Every source is joined onto the 1900 county map: the free-delivery panel by its county FIPS, Blevins & Helbock's post office gazetteer by coordinate, patents and the non-Southern lynchings by FIPS, the Southern lynchings by county name, and the census tables by NHGIS GISJOIN. Match rates for every join are printed in output/logs/g_county_panel*.log and none of them is silent.

Adoption was threshold-triggered, so the event chart is not a causal estimate. From July 1887 a town qualified for free city delivery with a population of 10,000 or gross receipts of $10,000, at the Postmaster General's discretion — before that the bar was 20,000 and $20,000. A county's adoption year is therefore mechanically correlated with how fast it was already growing, and the chart's rise after year zero partly reflects the growth that earned the service in the first place. The averages are also taken over an unbalanced set of counties, since which counties are observed at a given event time depends on when they adopted.

Two measures of violence, and they are not the same thing. Lynching is extralegal violence — the state failing to hold its monopoly. Prison and jail inmates are the state exercising it. If postal capacity marks the reach of the federal state, the two should move in opposite directions, and the tab lets you put them side by side. But read the mismatch first: lynching is overwhelmingly rural and Southern while free city delivery was urban by construction, so the two overlap less than the map suggests. The Southern lynching inventory is Beck & Tolnay's via the Project HAL compilation, because the authors' own host is currently unreachable.

What the inmate counts are, and what they are not. They are people enumerated in correctional institutions on census day: 49,116 in 1880, 71,406 in 1900 and 76,898 in 1920, spread over roughly 380 to 405 counties. That is 15–20% below the Census Bureau's own published prisoner counts, as a one-day group-quarters enumeration should be — it misses small jails holding nobody that night, and counts residents rather than commitments. Two things follow. Because the counts sit in only about one county in seven, this is closer to does this county hold a penal institution than to a local incarceration rate, and a county hosting a state penitentiary is not thereby a more punitive place. And note the direction: inmates rise 1.6× while population doubles, so incarceration per head slightly fell across the period even as the asylum and poorhouse population grew much faster.

Coverage and its gaps. Counts are annual; census measures exist only at benchmark years, and the map carries the most recent census that the measure was actually collected in — the caption always names which. Two gaps matter. There is no county manufacturing census for 1910, so establishments and value added skip from 1900 to 1920. And incarceration exists for 1880, 1900 and 1920 only: the 1910 census did not record the type of group quarters, coding prisons, asylums, hotels and boarding houses alike, so its inmates cannot be separated from its lodgers. Inmates are counted person by person in the complete-count census microdata, because no published county crime or prisoner series exists for this period — NHGIS has none across all 271 of its datasets, and its county vital statistics carry no cause of death. Urban share exists for 1900 alone. Alaska and Hawaii are dropped from this tab.

What the regressions say

Around the arrival of free delivery

What this tab is asking. If the post office is the federal government showing up in a county — a building, a salaried official, a daily connection to the national press and the national parties — then its arrival might reduce the appetite for parties organised against the centre. The map shows each party's county vote share across the eleven presidential elections from 1880 to 1920; the regression card reports what is left of the correlation once county and year fixed effects have absorbed how radical a place simply was and what was happening nationally.

There is no rural free delivery in this data, and that matters. Everything this project calls free delivery is free city delivery, from the Post Office Department's Item 95 statement: 974 of 2,844 counties, urban by construction, observed 1896–1910 only. The literature on mail and voting — Perlman and Sprick Schuster's Delivering the Vote — is about Rural Free Delivery, which reached the farm counties where Populism and agrarian socialism actually lived. So the workhorse treatment here is the extensive margin instead: post offices per 10,000 people, which is annual, universal, and covers every county in every year. Free city delivery is reported beside it as a second, urban margin, and it is the weaker test of the two.

None of these numbers is a causal estimate. Post offices were not sprinkled at random: they went where people were, and free city delivery was threshold-triggered on population and receipts from 1887, so its arrival is mechanically correlated with how fast a county was already growing. The fixed effects remove the part of that which is constant within a county or common to a year; they do not remove the rest. Read the coefficients as conditional correlations with a sign, and read the event study as a description of what happened around adoption rather than an estimate of what adoption did.

Which parties count as radical, and which the source will not separate. The insurgent-left aggregate is Greenback, People's and Socialist — the parties running against the prevailing economic order. Prohibition and the 1912 Progressives are deliberately not in it: both were anti-establishment, neither was anti-capitalist, and the Progressives are shipped separately so you can disagree. The real limit is the source. For 1880–1920 ICPSR 8611 carries only Democratic, Republican, Greenback, Populist, Socialist, Progressive and a residual other; Prohibition, Socialist Labor and Farmer-Labor are inside that residual and cannot be pulled out. So the radical-left aggregate is a floor, not a total, and 1888 and 1900 carry no minor-party column at all.

Three arithmetic warnings. The People's share collapses in 1896 because the party fused with the Democrats behind Bryan, not because the movement evaporated — that fusion vote is the separate Democratic (fusion) series, which is counted with the major parties because the platform was Bryan's. Turnout is ICPSR's, computed against the population estimated eligible to vote under the era's age, race, sex and citizenship rules, so the jump at 1920 is partly women entering the denominator rather than a surge of voting. And postal density is winsorised at its 99th percentile: a handful of frontier counties have an interpolated population in single digits, which turns one post office into two thousand per ten thousand people.
